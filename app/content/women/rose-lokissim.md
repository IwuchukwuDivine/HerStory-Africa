## Biography

Rose Lokissim was born in southern Chad around 1953 and became one of the first women to serve in the Chadian army. When Hissène Habré seized power and built a dictatorship on fear, she joined the armed opposition. In September 1984 she was captured by the Documentation and Security Directorate, Habré's dreaded secret police, and disappeared into a network of clandestine prisons where torture and death were routine.

## The Turning Point

Inside prisons like the converted swimming pool known as La Piscine, Lokissim did something extraordinary. Rather than simply survive, she resisted. She secretly documented the conditions and the names of the dead, smuggled messages out to prisoners' families, and even helped women giving birth in the cells. When the secret police discovered her covert work, they interrogated her one last time before executing her on 15 May 1986. Their own report recorded her final words of defiance: she had no regrets, she said, and even if they killed her, Chad would thank her and history would speak of her.

## Historical Context

Lokissim resisted during Habré's rule from 1982 to 1990, a Cold War regime whose secret police killed tens of thousands and tortured on an industrial scale. What she could not have known was how completely her prophecy would come true. In 2001 the abandoned files of the secret police were discovered, including the very report that quoted her last words, and those archives became crucial evidence when Habré was finally tried and convicted of crimes against humanity in 2016.

## Her Impact Today

Rose Lokissim is remembered in Chad as a national heroine of the anti-Habré resistance, a pioneering woman soldier who used her courage to protect others even as she faced death. Her story reached the world through the acclaimed documentary *Talking About Rose*, and the words her killers wrote down to condemn her instead ensured that history would remember her exactly as she said it would.
