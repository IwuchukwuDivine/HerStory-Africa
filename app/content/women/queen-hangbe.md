## Biography

Walk through the royal palaces at Abomey, in southern Benin, and you come to the display of recades, the carved bronze sceptres that stand in for each ruler of Dahomey. There is one for every king. There is a gap where hers should be.

That gap is the most honest thing anyone can show you about Hangbè. She was the daughter of Houegbadja and the twin sister of King Akaba, and Fon tradition says that when Akaba died on campaign she took power. The official king list of Dahomey, kept and recited at that same palace, runs straight from Akaba to Agaja as though the years between them did not happen. English-language reference works describe it as a particularly thorough case of damnatio memoriae, the Roman term for erasing someone from the record on purpose.

Even her dates are a casualty. The older chronology used in Benin puts her reign at 1708 to 1711. Historians working from European trading records, which fix Agaja's accession at 1718, put her in the gap between 1716 and 1718. Nobody can say for certain how long she held power, whether she ruled outright or as regent for her nephew, or in which year she was born or died. Some historians have gone further and questioned whether she existed at all.

She is not in the record because somebody took her out of it. That is the story.

## What tradition says happened

The scene the Abomey lineage has kept for three centuries is this. Akaba dies in the field against the Wémè. Telling the army would break it. So his twin sister puts on his armour and goes on commanding in his place, and the resemblance is close enough that it holds. The battle at Lissèzoun is won by a dead king who is actually his sister.

Tradition also keeps how it ended. The council ruled for her younger brother Agaja, and rather than accept it quietly she is said to have stripped in front of them and washed herself where they could see, an obscene and unanswerable statement of contempt. Her marriage and her two sons are said to have been casualties of the fight against her.

None of that is documented in the modern sense. No European had reached the court at Abomey when it happened. Europeans traded on the coast, and Dahomey was inland; the first European known to have got to Abomey was an English factor taken prisoner in 1724, a decade after her window closes on either chronology. What exists is oral tradition, court genealogy, and a family in Abomey that has never stopped saying it.

## Historical Context

Dahomey was a Fon kingdom on the Abomey plateau that grew, under Agaja and his successors, into a military state funded by selling captured people across the Atlantic. Succession normally passed through the male line. Hangbè is an exception to a stated rule, which is exactly why she was removable.

Women were not powerless there. The kpojito, usually translated as queen mother, was a real office with religious and political authority, and royal wives served as ministers. That makes the erasure sharper rather than stranger. And Dahomey did it again: King Adandozan, deposed in 1818, was struck off the same king list. Cutting a ruler out of the record was a tool the court knew how to use. Tradition says Agaja used it on his sister because a woman should not have held the throne. Some historians offer a colder reading, that a line descending from Hangbè and a line descending from Akaba would have split the dynasty, and one of them had to go. The two explanations are not in competition.

## What She Fought For

Her own succession, and the right of her nephew Agbo Sassa to follow her. She lost. What survived her was not a policy but a habit: armed women inside the palace, close to the king, trusted with weapons.

## Major Achievements

She is credited in Fon tradition with forming a female guard, and from there with the origin of the Mino, the women soldiers Europeans called the Dahomey Amazons. The claim deserves care, because it is the one thing the specialist literature will not sign off on. Stanley Alpern, who wrote the standard study of their origins, credits Agaja instead. The documented sequence is that a French slaver saw armed palace women at Ouidah in 1725, Agaja massed women in war dress in 1728 to make his army look bigger than it was, and the first written record of women in actual combat comes in 1729. Under Ghezo in the nineteenth century the corps grew to as many as six thousand, a third of the army, loading their guns in half the time male soldiers took.

So the honest version is not that she founded an army. It is that the practice everyone now recognises appears in Dahomey immediately after her, in the palace where she had ruled, and the people who kept her memory have always said that is not a coincidence.

## Her Impact Today

The title is alive. A woman in Abomey holds it now, near the royal palaces, presiding over the annual rites of the lineage, receiving visitors who prostrate and take a ceremonial sip of water, attended by four women who carry the name Amazons. One of them commissions a new parasol every year with "Reine Hangbe" worked into it. The filmmaker Arnaud Zohou, himself a descendant, filmed the handover in 2001, when the previous holder of the title was buried and her successor enthroned, the first such transition in sixty-seven years.

The wider world arrived late. The 2022 film The Woman King is set a century after her and does not include her, but the coverage around it pulled her name into English for the first time at scale. When Benin unveiled a thirty-metre bronze Amazon in Cotonou that same year, so many people assumed the face was hers that the government issued a statement saying it was not.

The official record still has a gap where her sceptre should be. Her family filled it themselves, and have been filling it every year for three hundred years.
