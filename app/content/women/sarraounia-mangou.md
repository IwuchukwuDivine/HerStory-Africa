## Biography

In the animist town of Lougou, in what is now south-central Niger, the ruler carried the title Sarraounia, a hereditary office that made a woman both queen and priestess. The Sarraounia of the late 19th century was named Mangou. Her people, the Azna, had spent generations resisting the powerful Muslim states around them and had kept their own gods, their own authority and their own stubborn independence.

## The Turning Point

In April 1899 the **Voulet-Chanoine mission** arrived, a French military column so brutal that it became a scandal even in Paris, leaving burned villages and massacred civilians across the region. When it reached Lougou, Sarraounia refused to submit. She armed her fighters and defended the town, and when French firepower proved overwhelming she did the thing that turned defeat into legend: rather than surrender or let her people be slaughtered, she withdrew them into the bush and switched to guerrilla warfare, harassing the column instead of dying in front of it. The French took an empty town and never subdued her. Weeks later the mission collapsed into mutiny and murder, its two commanders dead.

## Historical Context

In 1899 France was racing to stitch its West and Central African territories together, and the Voulet-Chanoine column was one of the instruments of that ambition, notorious for its atrocities. Sarraounia's small animist enclave met it not with panic but with a culture of resistance already generations deep. Hers was one of the few stands against the conquest led by a woman, and one of the few that the invaders could not turn into a victory.

## Legend and History

That a real Azna queen at Lougou resisted the French in 1899 and evaded capture is documented history. The supernatural halo around her, the claims that she had panther's eyes, could summon fog to hide her fighters or make crops regrow overnight, is oral legend, and it grew precisely because her real defiance was so striking. Her birth date, her appearance and her fate after 1899 are all unknown.

## Her Impact Today

Sarraounia became a national heroine of Niger and a pan-African emblem of anti-colonial and women's resistance. The Nigerien writer Abdoulaye Mamani turned her into the subject of his 1980 novel *Sarraounia*, and the Mauritanian director Med Hondo adapted it into a sweeping 1986 film that won the top prize at FESPACO. Her name is still invoked in Niger wherever courage and refusal are being celebrated.
