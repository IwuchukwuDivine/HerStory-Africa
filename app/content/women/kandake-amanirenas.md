## Biography

Kandake (Queen) Amanirenas ruled the Kingdom of Kush, centred in what is now northern Sudan, in the late 1st century BC. No surviving record gives the year she was born or the year she died. She was one of a long line of powerful Nubian queens, known as Kandakes, who ruled Kush as equals to or independently of their kings. The Greek geographer Strabo, writing from Rome's side, described her as "a masculine sort of woman, and blind in one eye." He never says how she lost the eye.

Her reign fell at a dangerous moment. The Roman Empire had recently conquered Egypt and was expanding southward along the Nile. When Roman forces threatened Kushite territory, Amanirenas did not wait to be attacked. She struck first.

## Historical Context

The Kingdom of Kush was one of Africa's most powerful and long-lived civilisations, lasting from approximately 1070 BC to 350 AD. Located along the Nile in present-day Sudan, Kush developed its own writing system (Meroitic script), built pyramids, and maintained sophisticated trade networks. The Kushites had a unique tradition of powerful queens, the Kandakes, who held political and military authority.

After Rome annexed Egypt in 30 BC following the death of Cleopatra, Roman garrisons pushed southward into Nubian territory, taxing local populations and threatening Kushite sovereignty. This set the stage for one of the ancient world's most remarkable military confrontations.

## What She Fought For

In 25 BC, while Rome's governor of Egypt was away fighting in Arabia, Kandake Amanirenas launched a military campaign against Roman-held territory in southern Egypt. Her forces attacked and captured the Roman garrison towns of Syene (modern Aswan), Elephantine, and Philae. They pulled down the statues of the Roman Emperor Augustus. The bronze head of one statue was torn from its body and carried south to Meroë. There it was buried beneath the steps of a temple to Victory, so that everyone who walked in walked over the emperor.

The Romans counterattacked under the general Gaius Petronius, pushing deep into Kushite territory and razing Napata, the royal city. Amanirenas regrouped and came back with thousands of soldiers against the Roman fort at Premnis (Qasr Ibrim), but Petronius got there first. How the war ended depends on who tells it. The Roman historian Cassius Dio says Petronius forced her to make terms. Strabo says her envoys were taken to Augustus on the Greek island of Samos and came away with everything they had asked for.

The resulting **Treaty of Samos** (around 21–20 BC) was remarkably favourable to Kush. Augustus cancelled the tribute Rome had imposed. The Romans pulled their garrison out of Premnis, and the border went back to roughly where the Ptolemies had drawn it, at Hiera Sycaminos. Kush returned many of the statues it had taken, though evidently not all of them. The kingdom kept its independence.

## Major Achievements

- Led Kushite armies against the Roman Empire, capturing Roman garrison towns in Upper Egypt
- Sent the envoys who secured the **Treaty of Samos**, which cancelled Rome's tribute and kept Kush independent
- One of the few rulers in the ancient world to successfully resist Roman expansion
- Took the statues of Augustus from Roman Egypt; the bronze head of one lay under a temple step at Meroë until 1910
- Maintained Kushite sovereignty for generations after the treaty

## Her Impact Today

Kandake Amanirenas is one of the most powerful examples of African resistance to foreign domination in ancient history. The bronze head of Augustus from the temple steps at Meroë was excavated by the archaeologist John Garstang in 1910 and now sits in the British Museum, still a symbol of Kushite defiance. Her own side of the story is probably carved on a sandstone stela from Hamadab, near Meroë, also in the British Museum. It names Amanirenas and Prince Akinidad, but it is written in Meroitic, a language scholars can sound out and still cannot read. Her story challenges the erasure of African civilisations from world history and demonstrates that Africa's tradition of powerful women leaders stretches back millennia.
