## Biography

Fatima Meer was born in Durban in 1928, the daughter of a newspaper editor, and she grew into one of the sharpest minds of South Africa's liberation struggle. Still a schoolgirl in 1946, she founded a resistance committee during the Indian Passive Resistance Campaign. In 1956 she became the first Black person appointed to a lecturing post at a white South African university, teaching sociology at the University of Natal, a position she held for more than thirty years.

## The Turning Point

In 1975 and 1976 Meer, with Winnie Mandela, co-founded the Black Women's Federation and became its first president. In August 1976, as the Soweto uprising shook the country, she was detained without trial and imprisoned alongside Winnie Mandela. Months later assassins petrol-bombed her home in an attempt to kill her. She survived, refused to be silenced, and emerged as one of the most prominent women in the entire movement, a scholar the apartheid state could ban and jail but could not frighten into silence.

## Historical Context

Meer worked at the seams of apartheid's racial hierarchy. The system sorted people into Indian, Coloured, African and white and worked hard to keep them divided. She spent her life insisting on the opposite, that Indian and African South Africans shared one struggle, and she paid for that conviction with repeated banning orders, detention and violence across four decades.

## Major Achievements

- First Black person to lecture at a white South African university
- Co-founded the Federation of South African Women and the Black Women's Federation, building durable Indian and African solidarity
- Wrote *Higher Than Hope*, the first authorised biography of Nelson Mandela, and more than forty other works
- Founded the Institute for Black Research and several schools and skills centres; honoured with the Order of Luthuli

## Her Impact Today

After 1994 Meer turned down a seat in the new democratic parliament, choosing grassroots education and community work over political office, exactly as she had lived. She is remembered as a rare figure who combined serious scholarship with physical courage, and who understood that the fight against injustice could not be won by one community alone.
