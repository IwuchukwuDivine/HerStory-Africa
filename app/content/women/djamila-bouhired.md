## Biography

Djamila Bouhired was born in Algiers in 1935 into a middle-class family, and she grew up under a French colonial system that treated Algerians as subjects in their own country. As a student she joined the National Liberation Front, the FLN, and became a liaison agent for its Algiers commander, moving through the narrow streets of the Casbah carrying messages and weapons during the fiercest phase of the war for independence.

## The Turning Point

In April 1957 she was captured. By her account she was tortured for seventeen days by French paratroopers, and she gave up nothing. In July she was tried in connection with a bombing and sentenced to death by guillotine. Her lawyer, Jacques Vergès, refused to argue a quiet case. He put the French state itself on trial, turning her name into an international cause and flooding the world's press with the story of a young woman facing execution for wanting her country to be free. The pressure worked. Her sentence was commuted, and in 1962 she walked out of prison as Algeria became independent.

## Historical Context

Bouhired fought in the urban heart of the Algerian War of Independence, when the FLN's bombing networks and France's systematic use of torture turned Algiers into a byword for the ugliness of colonial war. Her case did something the fighting alone could not: it forced the reality of French torture in front of a global audience and helped strip the occupation of its moral cover.

## Major Achievements

- Served as an FLN liaison agent through the Battle of Algiers
- Became a global symbol of anti-colonial struggle and of Arab and African women's militancy after her 1957 trial
- Her case, argued by Jacques Vergès, helped expose French torture in Algeria to the world
- Portrayed on screen in Youssef Chahine's *Jamila, the Algerian* (1958) and echoed in *The Battle of Algiers*

## Her Impact Today

Long after independence, Bouhired remained a moral reference point in Algeria, unafraid to criticise the governments that followed and lending her name to the 2019 protest movement in her eighties. She stands for a truth her captors tried to bury, that the people who carried Algeria to freedom included young women who walked into fire and refused to break.
