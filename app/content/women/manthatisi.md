## Biography

Manthatisi was born around 1781 near present-day Harrismith, in the borderlands of what is now South Africa and Lesotho. She married Mokotjo, chief of the Batlokwa, and might have lived out her life as a chief's wife. Instead, when Mokotjo died, she took power as regent for their young son Sekonyela and led her people directly into the most dangerous decades the region had ever known.

## The Turning Point

Rivals resisted the idea of a woman, and an outsider by birth, ruling the Batlokwa. Manthatisi answered them on the battlefield. Her most famous moment came when her fighting men were away and an enemy army approached her camp. With almost no soldiers left, she lined up the women of the community in ranks and put the few remaining men in front waving hoes and reed mats to imitate massed spears. From a distance it looked like a full army waiting to strike. The attackers withdrew. It was the perfect signature for a leader whose genius was making a small force appear overwhelming.

## Historical Context

Manthatisi ruled during the Difaqane, the great scattering of the 1810s to 1830s, when war, famine and mass migration tore across the southern African interior in the upheaval linked to the rise of the Zulu kingdom. In that collapse of older orders, leaders survived only by holding their followers together through force and cunning. A woman commanding a nation of tens of thousands at war was extraordinary, and her name spread so far that terrified neighbours used "the Mantatees" as a word for the invading hordes of the age.

## Legend and History

Because she was so feared, myth grew around her. Stories spread that she was a giant with a single eye in the middle of her forehead, that she fed her warriors from her own breast, that her people were cannibals. None of it is true. These were tales told by enemies and rivals, and Manthatisi may even have encouraged some of them to unsettle her opponents. The real woman was a capable administrator in peace as well as war, who held the Batlokwa together and secured the chieftaincy for her son.

## Her Impact Today

Her campaigns reshaped the region, pressuring the young Moshoeshoe toward the mountain stronghold where he would found the Basotho nation. Today the South African Navy's submarine SAS Manthatisi carries her name, schools are named for her, and she stands as one of the very few women who ruled and personally led a major African nation through war.
