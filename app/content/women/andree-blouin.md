## Biography

The medicine existed. It sat on a shelf, close enough to reach. But Andrée Blouin's two-year-old son René was burning with malaria, and under French colonial law, quinine was reserved for Europeans. The rules classified René as African, so the drug that could have saved him was locked away. He died. Blouin later wrote that she was an African woman "inflamed by injustice," and from that day she made the injustice pay.

She had already survived plenty. Born Andrée Madeleine Gerbillat on December 16, 1921, in Bessou, Oubangui-Chari, in what is now the Central African Republic, she was the daughter of Joséphine Wouassimba, a fourteen-year-old Banziri girl, and Pierre Gerbillat, a forty-year-old French businessman. At age three her father placed her in an orphanage for mixed-race girls run by nuns in Brazzaville, a place she called "a kind of waste bin for the waste products" of colonial society. She spent fourteen years there, hungry and beaten, teaching herself from books she had to steal. In 1938 she escaped when the nuns tried to push her into an arranged marriage.

Grief over René drove her into politics. She campaigned against the quinine law itself, then threw herself into the great independence movements of the 1950s. In Guinea she worked with Sékou Touré and the Rassemblement Démocratique Africain, mobilizing women village by village before the 1958 referendum on French rule. Guinea was the only French colony to vote No, and it became independent that October. Charles de Gaulle's government expelled her for it.

In 1960 she arrived in the Belgian Congo on the eve of its independence. Within one month she had enrolled 45,000 members in the Parti Solidaire Africain and was building a women's movement for health, literacy, and citizenship. Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba made her his chief of protocol, the only woman in his inner circle, and his speechwriter. The press nicknamed the pair "team Lumum-Blouin." When Lumumba's government was destroyed, she was forced out of Congo and sentenced to death. She lived the rest of her life in exile, first as a host to liberation movements in Algiers, then in Paris, where she died of lymphoma on April 9, 1986.

## Historical Context

Blouin's life spanned the entire arc of French and Belgian colonial rule in Central Africa, from its everyday cruelties to its collapse. Colonial society sorted people by race in everything: schools, jobs, marriages, even medicine. Children of African mothers and European fathers, like Andrée, were taken from their families and warehoused in institutions meant to keep them separate from both worlds. The quinine law that killed René was not an accident. It was policy.

After the Second World War, that order began to crack. Across French Africa, parties like the Rassemblement Démocratique Africain demanded a future without colonizers, and in 1958 de Gaulle offered the colonies a stark referendum: accept a French community, or take independence and lose all French support overnight. Guinea's lone No, which Blouin helped organize among women, electrified the continent.

The Congo she entered in 1960 was the most dangerous stage of all. Belgium had run the territory for profit and left it with almost no African doctors, officers, or administrators. Independence on June 30, 1960 was followed within weeks by mutiny, secession, and foreign interference, and within months by the overthrow and murder of Lumumba. Western newspapers, unable to imagine an African woman with real power, wrote about Blouin's looks and her mixed parentage instead of her politics.

## What She Fought For

Everything Blouin fought for leads back to a hospital shelf and a dying little boy. René's death taught her that colonialism was not an abstraction. It was a rule that said some children may have medicine and some may not. She began with the most concrete goal imaginable, changing the quinine law, and discovered that the whole system had to go.

Her weapon of choice was women. In Guinea, and later in Congo through the Feminine Movement for African Solidarity, she organized market women, mothers, and villagers into a political force, convinced, as she put it, that if she so chose, the woman could be the foremost instrument of independence. She talked about vaccination, literacy, and the vote in the same breath, because to her they were the same fight.

In Congo she worked at the very center of power. Congolese intelligence chief Victor Nendaka later testified that when Lumumba stood up on independence day, June 30, 1960, and delivered the speech that stunned the Belgian king, the version he chose to deliver was the one Blouin had drafted. She never claimed credit for it in public. She did the work, took the danger, and let the men take the stage.

She paid full price: expelled from Guinea, expelled from Congo, condemned to death by Lumumba's enemies, and written out of the story for decades. She kept organizing anyway, from Algiers and Paris, for the rest of her life.

## Major Achievements

- Campaigned against the colonial quinine law that reserved life-saving malaria medicine for Europeans, after it cost her two-year-old son René his life
- Helped mobilize Guinean women with Sékou Touré's RDA before the 1958 referendum, when Guinea became the only French colony to vote No and win immediate independence
- Enrolled 45,000 members in Congo's Parti Solidaire Africain in a single month in 1960 and built the Feminine Movement for African Solidarity
- Served as chief of protocol and speechwriter to Patrice Lumumba, the only woman in his inner circle, and drafted a version of his famous independence day speech
- Survived expulsion and a death sentence to become a hub for African liberation movements in exile in Algiers
- Wrote "My Country, Africa: Autobiography of the Black Pasionaria" (1983), one of the great firsthand accounts of Africa's independence era

## Her Impact Today

For decades Blouin was a footnote in other people's stories. That is changing fast. Johan Grimonprez's Oscar-nominated 2024 documentary "Soundtrack to a Coup d'État" put her words at the center of the Congo story, and in 2025 her memoir was reissued by Verso Books in an edition prepared by her daughter, Eve Blouin. A new generation is reading her account of the orphanage, of René, and of the two heady months when a woman from Bessou helped run a newly free Congo.

Her example matters because she connected things that are usually kept apart: public health and revolution, women's organizing and state power, personal grief and political strategy. Historians now cite her as proof that the independence era was never only a story of famous men.

Above all, she left a warning and a promise in one. A system that denies a child medicine because of his race can seem eternal, until someone inflamed by injustice decides otherwise. Blouin decided otherwise, and helped take down an empire.
