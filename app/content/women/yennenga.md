## Biography

Yennenga belongs to that rare space between history and myth where nations keep their founders. Tradition makes her a princess of the Dagomba kingdom of Dagbon, in what is now northern Ghana, and dates her somewhere in the medieval centuries. Her father, King Nedega, is said to have prized her above all his children, not for her beauty but for her skill in war. She could ride, throw a javelin and command troops, and she fought in her father's battles from her teens.

## The Turning Point

The problem, the story goes, was that her father loved her talents too much to let her go. He refused to let her marry, wanting to keep his warrior daughter at his side. So Yennenga made her point without words. She planted a field of wheat, then let the whole crop rot in the ground, and when her father saw the waste she told him this was what he was doing to her own life. Enraged, he locked her away. She escaped in the night dressed as a man, on horseback, and rode alone into the wilderness. There she met a solitary elephant hunter named Rialé, and their son, Ouedraogo, "stallion", became the ancestral founder of the Mossi kingdoms.

## Historical Context

The Mossi kingdoms that trace themselves to Ouedraogo became durable, cavalry-based states in the Volta basin, powerful enough to resist the great Muslim empires of Mali and Songhai to their north for centuries. Yennenga's story binds the Mossi to their southern cousins and explains the prestige of the horse and the warrior in Mossi identity. It is remembered less as biography than as the charter of a people.

## Her Impact Today

Few African women are honoured on this scale. The golden **Étalon de Yennenga**, the top prize at FESPACO in Ouagadougou, the largest film festival on the continent, carries her name. Burkina Faso's footballers are the Stallions in her memory, an equestrian statue of her stands in the capital, and a whole new town near Ouagadougou has been named Yennenga. More than eight centuries after she is said to have ridden out, her name still stands for courage, independence and the refusal to accept a smaller life.
