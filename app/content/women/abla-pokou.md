## Biography

Around the turn of the 18th century, a girl was born into the ruling Oyoko lineage of the Ashanti court at Kumasi, in what is now Ghana. Her name was Abla Pokou. She grew up inside one of the most powerful states in West Africa, a kingdom of gold, cavalry and ruthless royal politics, close enough to the throne to be swept up in its most dangerous game: the fight over who would rule next.

When the reigning Asantehene died, traditionally placed around 1750, a violent succession struggle broke out. Pokou's faction lost. To remain in Kumasi meant death, so she gathered her followers, servants, soldiers and kin, and led them west, away from Ashanti power, toward the country between the Comoé and Bandama rivers in present-day Côte d'Ivoire.

## The Turning Point

The story every Baoulé child still hears begins at the water. Pokou's people, exhausted and pursued, reached the Comoé River swollen in full flood, impossible to cross. As the legend tells it, a priest declared that the river spirits would open a path in exchange for the most precious thing among them, a noble child. Pokou gave up her own only son. Hippopotami rose to form a living bridge, the people crossed to safety, and on the far bank the grieving queen cried "Ba ouli", the child is dead. From that cry, tradition says, came the name of her people, the Baoulé.

## Historical Context

The 18th-century Ashanti Empire was an expanding, immensely wealthy Akan state, and losing factions in its succession disputes often marched off to found kingdoms of their own. Pokou's westward exodus was one of these migrations, carrying Akan people, language and gold-working traditions into the heart of modern Côte d'Ivoire long before any European power controlled the region.

## Legend and History

The core of the story is accepted as history: an Ashanti royal woman led a breakaway group west and founded the Baoulé in the mid-1700s. The famous details, the priest's demand, the sacrificed child, the bridge of hippos and the "Ba ouli" naming, belong to oral tradition and myth rather than the documentary record. Even the novelist Véronique Tadjo, who retold the tale, treats the sacrifice as legend. The power of the story lies in what it says about how a people remember their own beginning, through the image of a queen who lost everything to keep her people alive.

## Her Impact Today

The Baoulé are now among the largest ethnic groups in Côte d'Ivoire, and the country's first president, Félix Houphouët-Boigny, was one of them. Pokou lives on in classrooms, theatre and national memory as a symbol of sacrifice and nationhood. Véronique Tadjo's acclaimed novel *Reine Pokou* reimagined her story in 2004, and in 2013 she became the subject of *Pokou, princesse ashanti*, one of the first feature-length Ivorian animated films.
