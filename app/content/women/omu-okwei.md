## Biography

Okwei was born in 1872 in Ossomari, on the lower Niger River in present-day Anambra State, Nigeria, into Igbo royalty: daughter of Prince Osuna Afubeho, a wealthy warrior who owned trading and war canoes, and granddaughter of King Atanmaya Nzedegwu I of Ossomari. She had no formal schooling; at nine her mother sent her to live with Igala relatives, where she learned to trade fruits, yams and poultry. In 1889 she married Joseph Allagoa, a trader from Brass, against her family's wishes (they considered a trader beneath her royal rank), and though the marriage ended around 1890, it introduced her to European traders and African middlemen, connections she leveraged to start her own poultry trade. In 1895 she married Opene of Aboh, whose mother was herself a wealthy trader, and partnered with her mother-in-law before striking out on her own.

In 1904 she became an agent of the Niger Company, trading palm oil for European goods, one of very few African women in such a position. At her peak she held 400 trading "tickets," roughly 20,000 gallons of palm oil. During and after the First World War she diversified into tobacco, cotton goods, ivory and coral beads, plus money-lending to smaller traders and extensive real estate: she came to own canoe fleets, trucks and over a third of the riverside land at Onitsha. From 1912 into the 1930s she served on the Onitsha Native Court, one of the few women with a formal voice in colonial-era adjudication.

In August 1935 she was crowned Omu (Queen) of Ossomari, head of the Council of Mothers in the Igbo "dual-sex" governance system, responsible for women's affairs, markets and dispute settlement, with official residences in both Ossomari and Onitsha. Her network-building had a harder edge history should not gloss: she acquired pawned girls whom she placed as wives and mistresses of influential businessmen to extend her commercial reach, a practice of her era that modern readers must weigh honestly alongside her achievements. She died in 1943 in Onitsha, leaving about £5,000 in the bank and twenty-four houses. No Omu of Ossomari has been crowned since.

## Historical Context

Okwei's lifetime spans the British conquest and consolidation of Southern Nigeria: the Royal Niger Company era, the 1900 transfer to Crown rule, and full colonial administration. European firms monopolised import-export trade, making African intermediaries like Okwei indispensable. Igbo polities such as Ossomari traditionally practised "dual-sex" government, with a male Obi and a female Omu ruling women's spheres, an institution the British ignored and eroded. Okwei was its last great holder in Ossomari.

## What She Fought For

Economic power for African women within a colonial economy stacked against them. She proved that an Igbo woman could out-trade the intermediary class, bank the profits, and convert wealth into institutional authority. As Omu she defended women's traditional political power (the Council of Mothers, the markets, the right of women to govern their own commercial world) even as colonial rule was dismantling the very idea.

## Major Achievements

- Became an agent of the Niger Company in 1904, one of very few African women in such a position.
- Reached a peak trade of 400 tickets, roughly 20,000 gallons of palm oil, ranking among the wealthiest traders on the lower Niger.
- Appointed to the Onitsha Native Court in 1912, serving into the 1930s.
- Pioneered money-lending to smaller traders and built an empire of canoe fleets, trucks and Onitsha real estate.
- Crowned Omu of Ossomari in August 1935, the last person ever to hold the title.
- Died in 1943 leaving twenty-four houses in Onitsha and £5,000, an enormous estate for the time.

## Her Impact Today

Celebrated as Nigeria's "Merchant Queen," Okwei is a staple of Nigerian women's history, the standing proof that female enterprise on the Niger predated and outmanoeuvred colonialism. A statue marks her burial site at Ugolo Village, Ossomala, and her story anchors scholarship on the Igbo dual-sex political system: a reminder that African women once held constitutional power that colonial rule erased, and that its memory is itself a form of resistance.
