## Biography

Destiny Ayo Vaughan was born in Nigeria around 1999. Before she was old enough to understand the word, her marriage had already been arranged. Then a different plan arrived first. She and her sisters were told they were going to Ireland, to school, to a better life. She was five.

There was no school. The house they were brought to became a prison. For the next five years the sisters were kept inside, hidden from neighbours, teachers, and anyone who might have asked questions. Attempts to leave were punished with pepper rubbed into their eyes.

She was eventually removed from the house and placed in the Irish foster care system. It was safer than captivity, but it was not a homecoming. She has spoken about bullying, instability, and the particular isolation of being a Black child in a system that had not been built with her in mind.

What she did next is the reason her name belongs in this archive. She went back to the classroom she had been denied and did not leave it. A Level Five qualification in Early Childhood Education. A Bachelor's Honours degree in Social Science. A Master's in Psychological Studies from the University of Aberdeen, on scholarship. Then a further scholarship for a Master's in Clinical Health Psychology at Queen's University Belfast.

## Historical Context

Nigeria is consistently named among the main countries of origin for people trafficked into Europe. Much of that trafficking does not look like a stranger with a van. It looks like a relative or family friend offering a child a future abroad. The promise of education is one of the oldest lures in the trade, because it is the one that parents most want to believe.

Ireland, for its part, has spent the past two decades reckoning with how its institutions treat the children in their care. Survivors of the country's residential institutions, its mother and baby homes, and its foster system have forced a slow public conversation about what happens to children after the state steps in. Destiny's story lands inside that conversation: a child rescued from one form of harm and delivered into a system that struggled to protect her from others.

At the same time, Ireland's public reckoning with sexual violence was gathering pace. High-profile trials in the late 2010s put the treatment of complainants under a harsh spotlight, and survivors were increasingly vocal about why they had never reported. Silence, they said, was safer.

## What She Fought For

Destiny's central conviction is that survivors stay silent because they do not feel safe, and that safety can be designed.

In 2019 she founded Mind the Gap Ireland, the country's first anonymous story-sharing platform for survivors of sexual violence. Survivors do not have to show a face or give a name. They tell what happened, in their own words, and are heard. The organisation funds therapy through donations, runs public events, and uses social media to reach survivors where they already are.

She also fights for the child she was. Her TEDx talk at TEDxTraleeWomen, "Understanding the Impact of Trauma on Children," draws on her own experience to explain how early trauma reshapes a person's sense of self, and what recovery actually requires. She speaks internationally on mental health, sexual violence, diversity, resilience, and entrepreneurship.

## Major Achievements

- Founded Mind the Gap Ireland in 2019, now supporting hundreds of survivors of sexual violence every year.
- Mind the Gap Ireland won the Tech and Innovation Award at the 2025 Eir Women of the Year Awards.
- Named Activist of the Year at the Black and Irish Gala Awards.
- Listed among the Irish Independent's "50 Ones to Watch" in 2023.
- Selected as a delegate to the United Nations Commission on the Status of Women, and as a Local Pathways Fellow under the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network in 2022.
- Invited to the 2024 Forbes Under 30 Summit Africa in Botswana, and put forward as a candidate for the Forbes 30 Under 30 Europe list.
- Featured in Dr. Sam Collins' book *Rebellious*, and represented by the London Speaker Bureau.
- Earned two university degrees on scholarship after being denied schooling as a child, then won a third scholarship for postgraduate study.

## Her Impact Today

Most stories about trafficking end at the rescue. Destiny's begins there. She has built, in a country that once hid her, a place where other hidden people can be heard. Every anonymous story posted on Mind the Gap Ireland is a small reversal of the logic that kept her behind a locked door: that a girl like her had no voice worth hearing.

Her degrees are part of the same work. She chose to study trauma from the inside, with academic rigour, and to bring that combination onto stages where policy is discussed. Universities, conferences, and UN forums now hear about child trafficking and foster care from someone who lived both.

She is still in her twenties. The archive usually waits decades to add a name. Hers is here because the story is already changing how a country talks about its survivors, and because girls who are told their futures have been decided deserve to know that one of them said no, out loud, and was believed.
