On Monday, 8 July 2002, about 150 women from Ugborodo took a Chevron boat across the water to the Escravos oil terminal. None of them worked for Chevron. The youngest were about thirty and the oldest about ninety. They carried bundles of food, and not one of them carried a weapon.

Then they refused to leave.

## The village next to the oil

Escravos sits where the Escravos River meets the Atlantic, in the western Niger Delta, not far from Warri in Delta State. The name is Portuguese for "slaves". Chevron's main base in Nigeria is here: tank farms, docks, a helipad and an airstrip, ringed by kilometres of creeks and swamp. Oil from the company's fields in the west of the Delta is stored at Escravos before it is shipped out.

Ugborodo, an Itsekiri community, sits right beside it. In 2002 its women wanted things that sound almost modest: jobs for their sons, electricity and clean water. Their spokeswoman, Anunu Uwawah, told reporters that apart from one village with a company office, everyone in the area lived without electricity. The boat to Warri took three hours.

## Every way in, every way out

The women went for the places that mattered. They took the docks, the helipad and the airstrip, the only ways on or off the terminal, and they barricaded the tank farm. Boats could not dock. Planes could not land.

More than 700 workers were inside, Americans, Britons and Canadians among them. No one was hurt, and no one was allowed to leave. The oil stopped moving.

Chevron's spokesman, Wole Agunbiade, chose his words with care. "This is not a hostage situation," he told the Canadian Press. "This is an occupation."

## The threat

About a hundred police and soldiers were sent to the terminal. They did not harm the women.

The women had made sure everyone knew what would happen if anyone attacked them. They would take off their clothes.

In the Niger Delta, a mother or a grandmother stripping in protest is a curse, kept for the worst of times. The shame falls on whoever drove her to it. The historian [Bolanle Awe](/women/bolanle-awe) put it plainly to the Christian Science Monitor that summer: "By the time the women bare their chests and go around, people are really in trouble."

The threat was enough.

## Ten days

The occupation grew. Women from the communities around Warri, most of them Itsekiri, came to join it, and by some counts the 150 became about 2,000. On the first Sunday the women let several hundred workers go, as a show of good faith. On 16 July they told the BBC the deal was off, because Chevron's negotiators were not being sincere. Uwawah was clear about how this would end: "We stay today, but once the paper is signed, we will leave."

The paper was signed on 17 July. Chevron agreed to hire more than two dozen villagers, to build a town hall in Ugborodo and new schools, to bring in electricity and water, and to help villagers set up fish and poultry farms that would supply the terminal's own cafeteria. On 18 July, the women went home.

## It spread

Other women were watching. While Escravos was still occupied, Ijaw women from the Gbaramatu area took four Chevron flow stations by boat, and came away with promises of jobs, a school, electricity and water. Amnesty International counted more than a thousand women at Chevron's office near Warri on 29 July.

Not every gate opened so easily. In August, when hundreds of Itsekiri, Ijaw, Ilaje and Urhobo women gathered outside the Shell and Chevron offices in Warri, soldiers broke up the protest with tear gas, shots in the air and beatings. Eyewitnesses told Human Rights Watch that at least one woman was shot and badly wounded. When Amnesty International visited in October, women in their seventies and eighties were still showing the scars.

## A war women had fought before

When reporters went looking for a precedent, they found one. In 1929, women across southeastern Nigeria went to war with the colonial government. They summoned each other with palm fronds, "sat on" the warrant chiefs and tore down the native courts. Nigerians call it [Ogu Umunwaanyi](/articles/the-aba-womens-war), the women's war.

Seventy-three years later, in the creeks of the western Delta, a different generation of women reached for the same kind of power. There were no guns in it. There was only the sight of all of them together, and the knowledge that no one wanted to be the man who laid a hand on somebody's grandmother.

The deal stayed in their lives for years. In 2020, ThisDay reported, Ugborodo women who supplied food to the terminal under that 2002 agreement occupied Chevron's tank farm again, this time over unpaid bills.

They had done it before. They knew the way in.
